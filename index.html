Spot a fake ministry email in 15 minutes

A hands-on lab on email header forensics. You will read the hidden “postal stamps” on an email, test who really sent it, and decide whether to block it or escalate it. No tools to install; everything runs in this page.

Level: beginner. Time: about 30 minutes. Safe: all data here is fictional.

The raw concept

In plain wordsAn email is like a parcel. Anyone can write any return address on the outside (the From line). But every post office the parcel passes adds its own stamp (the Received lines). To catch a fake, read the stamps, not the label.

Every email carries a hidden header: a log of where it came from, which servers handled it, and whether those servers passed three identity checks.

CheckQuestion it answersPlain-language meaning
SPFIs the sending server on the domain’s approved list?Is this courier allowed to deliver for this company?
DKIMDoes the message carry a valid digital signature?Is the wax seal intact and genuine?
DMARCDoes the visible From domain match, and what should happen if it fails?Does the label match the sender, and what is the rule if not?
Received linesWhich servers touched it, in which order?The postmarks. Read them bottom to top.
Important limit. A header can show where a message technically came from. It cannot prove that a foreign defence body sent it. Attackers rent servers, VPNs and hacked machines abroad on purpose. Treat your findings as indicators and let your CERT make attribution decisions.

The scenario: the fake ministry mail

An officer at a defence office receives an urgent email that appears to be from a foreign ministry’s procurement desk. It asks them to open Tender_Document.docx and reply with their staff list before Friday. The officer does not open it and forwards it to you as a .eml file. Your task: decide whether it is spam, phishing, or a targeted attack, and write a short triage note.

Here is the header you received. Study it, then work through the steps. (All names and IP addresses are fictional.)

Return-Path: <bounce@mailer77.hostbulk.example>
Received: from mail.agency.example (10.1.1.5) by mx.agency.example; Tue, 17 Nov 2026 06:12:20 +0530
Received: from relay2.hostbulk.example (relay2.hostbulk.example [198.51.100.12]) by mail.agency.example; Tue, 17 Nov 2026 06:12:18 +0530
Received: from unknown-host (unknown [203.0.113.45]) by relay2.hostbulk.example; Tue, 17 Nov 2026 03:12:10 +0300
Authentication-Results: mx.agency.example; spf=fail smtp.mailfrom=hostbulk.example; dkim=none; dmarc=fail header.from=rnod-gov.example
From: "Ministry of Defence Procurement" <procurement@rnod-gov.example>
Reply-To: <tender.desk.2026@freemailbox.example>
Subject: URGENT: Confidential tender, reply today
Date: Tue, 17 Nov 2026 03:12:09 +0300
Message-ID: <7f3a91@mailer77.hostbulk.example>

Steps to solve it

Progress: 0 of 7 steps

1

Do not open anything

Do not click links, open the attachment or reply. Save the original as a .eml file and record its hash so it can serve as evidence.

Get-FileHash C:\Case05\suspect.eml -Algorithm SHA256
How to do it
  1. Save the mail as a file. Gmail: open the mail, click the three dots (top right of the message), choose Download message. Thunderbird: right-click the mail, Save As, keep the .eml type. Outlook (classic): drag the mail to a folder, which saves a .msg file, which is fine for evidence.
  2. Create the case folder. Open PowerShell and run mkdir C:\Case05, then move the saved file into it and name it suspect.eml.
  3. Hash it. Run the command above. Copy the long SHA256 value into a notepad file with today’s date and time. This proves later that the file never changed.
  4. Work on a copy. Run Copy-Item C:\Case05\suspect.eml C:\Case05\work.eml and use work.eml from here on. Keep the original untouched.
2

Compare the three addresses

Look at From, Return-Path and Reply-To. Honest mail usually keeps them on the same organization.

How to do it
  1. Open the full header. Gmail: three dots, Show original. Outlook (classic): double-click the mail so it opens in its own window, then File, Properties, and read the Internet headers box at the bottom. Thunderbird: View, Headers, All (or press Ctrl+U).
  2. Or use PowerShell:
    Select-String -Path C:\Case05\work.eml -Pattern '^(From|Return-Path|Reply-To):'
  3. Write down only the part after the @ sign in each of the three lines. That part is the domain.
  4. Compare them. Same domain or the same organization: normal. Three different domains, or a free mailbox such as Gmail or Yahoo in Reply-To for an “official” mail: red flag.
  5. Look closely at spelling. Swapped letters like rn for m, an extra hyphen, or a different ending such as .co instead of .com are lookalike tricks.
Show the answerFrom says rnod-gov.example (note “rn” instead of “m”, a lookalike). Return-Path is hostbulk.example, a bulk mailer. Reply-To is freemailbox.example, a free mailbox. Three different domains: strong red flag.
3

Read the identity checks

Find the Authentication-Results line and read SPF, DKIM and DMARC.

How to do it
  1. Gmail is the easiest. In Show original, the box at the top lists SPF, DKIM and DMARC with PASS or FAIL next to each.
  2. Outlook or any other client: open the Internet headers, press Ctrl+F, and search for Authentication-Results. You will see text like spf=fail; dkim=none; dmarc=fail.
  3. Or use PowerShell:
    Select-String -Path C:\Case05\work.eml -Pattern 'Authentication-Results|Received-SPF|dkim|dmarc'
  4. Read the result words.
    WordMeaning
    passCheck succeeded
    fail or softfailServer not approved or signature broken: suspicious
    none or missingNo protection published: no proof either way
  5. Check which domain passed. In the line, look at header.from=. It must match the visible From domain. A pass for a different domain (the attacker’s own mailer) proves nothing.
  6. Shortcut: paste the header into the Analyzer below and it reads all three for you.
Show the answerspf=fail, dkim=none, dmarc=fail. The sending server was not approved to send for the claimed domain, and nothing was signed. This is classic spoofing.
4

Trace the route (bottom to top)

The bottom Received line is closest to the true sender. List the public IP addresses. Ignore private ranges (10.x, 192.168.x, 172.16 to 31.x).

How to do it
  1. Find every line that starts with Received: in the header. Each one is a server that handled the mail.
  2. Read from the bottom line up. The bottom line is the first server that accepted the mail, so it is closest to the real sender. The top line is your own server.
  3. In each line find the IP address, usually inside square brackets, such as [203.0.113.45].
  4. Cross out private addresses. These start with 10., 192.168., 127., or 172.16 to 172.31. They are inside networks and cannot be looked up.
  5. Or automate it with the PowerShell command below. It prints every IP found; then remove the private ones yourself.
  6. Optional visual view: Google Admin Toolbox Messageheader shows each hop and the delay between hops. It is a public website, so use it only for practice headers, never for sensitive real ones.
$h = Get-Content C:\Case05\suspect.eml -Raw
[regex]::Matches($h,'\b(?:\d{1,3}\.){3}\d{1,3}\b').Value | Select -Unique
Show the answerOrigin: 203.0.113.45 (bottom). Next hop: 198.51.100.12 (relay2.hostbulk.example). 10.1.1.5 is a private internal address, so ignore it. The origin host is named “unknown-host”, with no proper identity.
5

Look up the origin IP and the domain

Check the country and network owner of the origin IP, and the age of the sender’s domain. For sensitive cases use your organization’s internal tools, not public sites.

How to do it
  1. Look up the IP. In a browser open ipinfo.io and type the IP into the search box, or in PowerShell run Invoke-RestMethod https://ipinfo.io/8.8.8.8/json and replace 8.8.8.8 with your IP. Note the country and the org (the network owner).
  2. Judge the owner. A hosting company, VPN or cloud provider sending “ministry” mail is suspicious. A real ministry usually sends from its own mail servers.
  3. Check the domain age. Download Sysinternals Whois from Microsoft, then run whois rnod-gov.example and read the Creation Date. A domain created days ago that imitates an official body is a strong red flag.
  4. Check its mail rules.
    Resolve-DnsName rnod-gov.example -Type TXT
Resolve-DnsName _dmarc.rnod-gov.example -Type TXT
    Look for text starting with v=spf1 and v=DMARC1. No records means the domain is not protected, or does not exist.
  5. Practice note: the lab’s IPs and domains are fictional, so real lookups return nothing. Practice the commands with 8.8.8.8 and a real domain of your own first.
  6. Real cases: use your SOC’s threat-intelligence platform instead of public sites for sensitive headers.
Resolve-DnsName rnod-gov.example -Type TXT
Resolve-DnsName _dmarc.rnod-gov.example -Type TXT
Show the answerIn a real case you would use WHOIS and an IP lookup (ipinfo, AbuseIPDB) here. Expect a hosting or VPN provider and a domain registered only days ago. A foreign origin does not prove a foreign government sent it.
6

Check the time clues

Compare the Date header’s time zone with the claimed sender and with your own server’s timestamps.

How to do it
  1. Find the Date: line. The last part, such as +0300, is the sender’s time zone, three hours ahead of UTC.
  2. Find the timestamp on your own server’s Received line (the top one), for example +0530.
  3. Convert both to UTC with PowerShell:
    [datetimeoffset]::Parse('Tue, 17 Nov 2026 03:12:09 +0300').UtcDateTime
[datetimeoffset]::Parse('Tue, 17 Nov 2026 06:12:20 +0530').UtcDateTime
  4. Compare. If the sender’s time is later than your server received it, or hours away, the clock is wrong or forged.
  5. Ask: does the zone fit the story? A mail claiming to be from your own country but sent in a time zone far away is a hint only. Attackers can set any clock, so never decide on this alone.
Show the answerDate shows +0300 at 03:12, while your server logged +0530 at 06:12. They are the same moment, so that part is consistent. The time zone only hints at the sender’s system clock, and attackers can set it to anything. Use it as a hint, never as proof.
7

Decide and report

Choose a category, an action, and write a five-line triage note.

How to do it
  1. Add up your findings. Many red flags in steps 2 to 5 (domains differ, checks fail, new domain, hosting IP) point to phishing or spoofing. Targeted staff, secrecy and an attachment point to spear phishing. Everything matching and passing means probably genuine, but still confirm by phone.
  2. Write the five-line note: 1) what arrived and when, 2) domains and IPs seen, 3) which checks failed, 4) your category and why, 5) what you did next. Paste the file hash under it.
  3. Contain it. Ask your mail admin to block the sender domain and the sending IP, and to search all mailboxes for the same subject. With Exchange Online admin rights: 
    Get-MessageTrace -SenderAddress procurement@rnod-gov.example -StartDate (Get-Date).AddDays(-2) -EndDate (Get-Date)
  4. Warn the people who received it: do not open, do not reply, delete after your admin collects a copy.
  5. Escalate. Send the .eml, the hash and your note to your SOC or CERT (CERT-In in India). Keep the original file safe.
Show the answerLikely spear phishing. It targets named staff, uses secrecy and urgency, spoofs a ministry, and carries an attachment. Action: block the sender and sending IP, preserve the .eml and hashes, warn recipients, and escalate to your SOC or CERT (CERT-In in India). Do not reply.
